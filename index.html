About the track

This track presents work on artificial intelligence for systems that are coupled to the physical world through sensors, actuators, or both. It covers the sensing side, where inference must run on constrained and networked hardware, and the acting side, where decisions carry physical consequences that cannot be undone. Such systems can be viewed as embodied AI agents: agents that are physically situated in, interact with, and act upon the real world through robots, sensors, actuators, mobile devices, and other connected objects.

The two sides share a set of difficulties that general AI venues treat as secondary: energy, memory, and bandwidth budgets that bound what can be computed at all; perception that drifts, fails, and disagrees with itself; timing requirements set by the process being observed or controlled; and a distance between benchmark performance and deployed behavior that is wider than in most areas of AI.

Robotics and the Internet of Things are usually treated as separate fields with separate venues, and their researchers rarely read each other. That separation is becoming difficult to defend. Robots are increasingly networked fleets that share models and telemetry, and their onboard compute is subject to the same energy and thermal limits that constrain a sensor node. IoT deployments increasingly actuate rather than only report, which gives them the property that made robotics difficult in the first place: actions with consequences.

What the two have in common is that the physical world sets the terms. A model that is accurate but too large for the device does not ship. A policy that is optimal in simulation and unsafe on hardware is not a result. A prediction with unstated confidence, drawn from a sensor that may have failed silently, is not usable evidence for a decision.

The track gives that work a home at FLAIRS, with reviewers who will evaluate it on its own terms rather than reading a resource-cost result as a missing accuracy result. It is deliberately broad across hardware scale, from microcontrollers to mobile manipulators, and across application domains, on the view that the underlying difficulty is structural rather than domain specific. Contributions may be theoretical, algorithmic, or empirical.

What the track encourages

Efficiency claims in this literature are often asserted from parameter counts or estimated operation counts rather than measured, and autonomy claims are often supported by simulation alone. The track encourages:

  • results obtained on physical hardware or on realistic deployment traces, with resource cost reported alongside accuracy;
  • stated measurement methodology for energy, latency, memory, and safety;
  • released code, traces, and configurations;
  • honest accounting of how much human supervision a system actually received;
  • reporting of failure cases, negative results, and replications.

Submissions that carry a method across the boundary between the sensing and acting communities, in either direction, are especially welcome.

Topics

Papers are invited on any work relating to artificial intelligence in systems that sense or act on the physical world, whether the platform is a microcontroller, a sensor network, an edge gateway, a vehicle, or a robot. Topics of interest include, but are not limited to:

  • Inference under resource constraints On-device and edge inference for robots and IoT devices; quantization, pruning, distillation, and other compression for constrained targets; neural architecture search for microcontroller and accelerator targets; energy-aware model and algorithm selection; early-exit, cascaded, and hierarchical inference across device, gateway, and cloud tiers; measurement and modeling of inference energy, latency, and memory.
  • Foundation models and world models for embodied agents World models and foundation models for embodied AI agents; language-conditioned and vision-language-action policies; learning, perception, and reasoning for physical agents; grounding and adapting large pretrained models on platforms with limited compute; AI-enabled sensing and actuation.
  • Learning over sensor and robot data Representation learning and self-supervised objectives for sensor time series; multimodal and cross-sensor fusion; transfer and cold-start learning across deployments, devices, and platforms; handling sensor drift, faults, missing data, and label scarcity; forecasting, segmentation, and event detection on device-generated signals; learning from demonstration and from logged operational data.
  • Perception, planning, and control for embodied systems Perception in service of action; localization, mapping, and navigation, including SLAM; manipulation and mobility; planning and decision making under uncertainty for autonomous robots and IoT systems, including MDP, POMDP, and decentralized POMDP formulations; task and motion planning; reinforcement learning for control, including safe and constrained variants; execution monitoring and recovery.
  • Distributed and networked physical systems Multi-robot and swarm systems; robot-to-IoT collaboration and coordination among embodied agents; task allocation among devices or robots; robotics systems and architectures; communication protocols for robots and IoT devices; federated, split, and decentralized learning across fleets; communication-efficient training and update compression; personalization under device and platform heterogeneity; scheduling and placement of computation across tiers.
  • Uncertainty, trust, and provenance Uncertainty quantification and calibration for sensor-derived predictions; conformal and evidential methods in sensing and control pipelines; trust and reputation modeling over device and data-source networks; fusion of conflicting or unreliable observations; anomaly and fault detection with stated reliability characteristics; provenance, semantic interoperability, and knowledge representation for device data.
  • Safety, security, privacy, and governance Safety and verification for systems that act physically; formal guarantees and other system properties; privacy-preserving inference and analytics over device data; adversarial attacks and defenses in sensing and control pipelines; intrusion and anomaly detection and resilience for connected systems; human oversight and interruptibility; auditability and regulatory compliance for automated decisions taken over machine-generated evidence; ethics and societal implications of robotics and embodied AI.
  • Transfer, deployment, and applications Transfer from simulation to physical hardware; digital twins; human-robot and human-device interaction, shared autonomy, and teleoperation; field and deployment reports from systems operating outside the laboratory; applications across health and wellbeing, energy and smart grids, agriculture, transport, manufacturing, smart buildings, and environmental monitoring.
  • Methodology and reproducibility Benchmarks, datasets, testbeds, and simulation environments; reproducible energy, latency, and safety measurement methodology; reproducibility of results obtained on hardware; negative and replication results.

Work on agents and multi-agent systems in which the agents are robots or physical devices is in scope. Submissions whose primary contribution concerns software agents with no physical coupling, including coordination, planning, and orchestration among agents that neither sense nor actuate, fall outside this track; the FLAIRS-40 general conference, or a special track on agentic AI and multi-agent systems if one is offered, is the right destination for that work. If you are unsure whether your paper fits, write to the chairs before the abstract deadline.

Who should submit

The track is deliberately broad across hardware scale and application domain. It is written for, and expects submissions from:

  • Researchers working on machine learning for embedded, edge, and sensing systems, including those who publish at IEEE WF-IoT, in the IEEE Internet of Things Journal, at ACM SenSys, IPSN, EWSN, and the ACM/IEEE Symposium on Edge Computing, and in the tinyML and embedded machine learning communities.
  • Robotics researchers working on autonomy, perception for action, multi-robot systems, and learning for control, including those who publish at ICRA, IROS, RSS, and CoRL and are looking for a venue where the AI content of the work is the main object of review.
  • Researchers in efficient and resource-aware machine learning, model compression, federated learning, and neural architecture search for constrained targets.
  • Researchers in uncertainty quantification, sensor fusion, and trust modeling who want application settings in which calibration has operational consequences.
  • Industry practitioners deploying inference or autonomy on device and robot fleets, for whom deployment experience reports and negative results are of direct value.
  • Students and early-career researchers. Both halves of this area admit well-designed studies on affordable hardware, and the track welcomes small, carefully measured contributions alongside larger ones.

Submission

Submit through the FLAIRS-40 EasyChair site and select the Embodied AI Agents, Robotics, and IoT track. Submission is a two-step process: register the paper abstract by January 22, 2027, then upload the PDF by February 1, 2027. A paper cannot be submitted without an abstract registered on time.

Paper types

  • Full paper. Mature novel research, up to 6 pages excluding references at submission. Accepted full papers appear with up to 7 pages in the proceedings and receive a 20-minute oral presentation.
  • Short paper. Work of some novelty and general interest that is preliminary or at an early stage, up to 4 pages excluding references. Accepted short papers appear with up to 5 pages and receive a 10-minute oral presentation.
  • Poster paper. Up to 2 pages excluding references, presented in the poster session. Poster papers have a later deadline; see the dates above.

A rejected full paper may still be accepted as a short paper or poster when reviewers find the idea interesting but the paper is not ready for full-length publication.

Format and review

Papers must use the FLAIRS-40 template and be submitted as PDF. Reviewing is double-blind, so submissions must not carry author names or affiliations. Appendices placed after the references are permitted at submission but may not be reviewed, and they are not permitted in the final version. Papers are reviewed by the track program committee, which is sized so that each paper can receive four reviews. Papers by the track chairs are reviewed by the general conference program committee, with decisions made by the FLAIRS-40 program co-chairs.

Policies

FLAIRS will not accept a paper that is under review for, or already published or accepted in, a journal or another conference, and authors may not submit the paper elsewhere during the review period. Workshops and similar presentations without archival proceedings are exempt. Author names may not be changed or reordered after reviewing. Every accepted paper needs at least one author registration, which covers up to two papers, and at least one author must attend the conference in person to present. Proceedings are published by Florida Online Journals and indexed in DBLP and Scopus.
